<h1><strong>LLC vs. LLP: What's the Difference?</strong></h1>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The main difference between an LLC and an LLP is who can use each one and how far the liability protection goes.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A limited liability company (LLC) works for one owner or many, and most states let almost any business form one.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A limited liability partnership (LLP) is a general partnership that registers for liability protection.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">It needs at least two partners, and some states reserve it for licensed professionals such as lawyers, accountants, and architects.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Both structures keep business income from being taxed twice, and both can shield your personal assets in some situations.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The gaps show up in the details: who the shield covers, which states allow the structure, how much paperwork it needs, and what happens when a partner makes a mistake.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">This guide covers each one so you can match the structure to your business. Facts below carry a source and a date, and state rules change, so confirm the details with your state's business office before you file.</span></p>
<h2><strong>The Short Answer</strong></h2>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>LLC:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Open to a single owner or a group. The SBA says LLCs protect you from personal liability in most instances.&nbsp;</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Taxed as a pass-through by default. Available in every state for most kinds of business.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>LLP:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Requires at least two partners. Each partner is protected from the other partners' actions, though the shield on business debts varies by state.&nbsp;</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Often limited to licensed professions. Taxed as a partnership.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Quick rule:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Most small businesses, freelancers, and online sellers pick an LLC. Law firms, accounting firms, and similar practices often consider an LLP where their state allows it.</span></li>
</ul>
<h2><strong>What Is an LLC?</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">An LLC is a business structure that separates you from your company. If the business gets sued or can't pay a debt, creditors generally go after the company's assets, not your house or savings.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The U.S. Small Business Administration puts it this way: "LLCs protect you from personal liability in most instances" (SBA, choose a business structure, page updated August 28, 2026). The SBA also lists LLC ownership as one or more people.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">LLCs have a loose structure. Owners, called members, can run the company themselves or hire managers.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">They can split profits in whatever proportions they agree on in an operating agreement, and they aren't required to hold the formal meetings that corporations do.</span></p>
<h3><strong>How an LLC is taxed</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The IRS classifies a domestic LLC by its number of members. With two or more members, it is taxed as a partnership by default.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">With one member, it is treated as separate from its owner for income tax purposes, though it still counts as a separate entity for employment taxes and certain excise taxes.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">An LLC can also file Form 8832 to choose corporate tax treatment instead (IRS, limited liability company page, reviewed October 10, 2026).</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The SBA adds that LLC members count as self-employed and owe self-employment tax toward Medicare and Social Security, which surprises many first-time owners.</span></p>
<h2><strong>What Is an LLP?</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">An LLP is a general partnership that files with the state to gain liability protection.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Instead of forming a new kind of company from scratch, two or more partners register their existing partnership as an LLP.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The SBA explains that an LLP "protects each partner from debts against the partnership" and that partners aren't responsible for one another's actions.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">That second point is the heart of the LLP. In a regular general partnership, every partner can be personally liable for what the others do.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">An LLP removes most of that risk, which is why professional firms adopted it. According to the Journal of Accountancy, the AICPA changed its Rule 505 in 1992 to let accountants practice through LLCs and LLPs after years of heavy litigation.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">That article dates from September 2005, so use it for background and check your state's current statute for the details.</span></p>
<h3><strong>Full shield vs. partial shield</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Not every LLP gives the same protection. States fall into two groups, according to that article:</span></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Full shield:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> A partner is not liable for other partners' malpractice, and is also shielded from the partnership's contract debts and ordinary business obligations.&nbsp;</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">A landlord or supplier can reach only the partnership's assets.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Partial shield:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> A partner is protected from other partners' malpractice but stays personally liable for the partnership's contract obligations and ordinary business debts.</span></li>
</ul>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">In both cases, you remain responsible for your own mistakes. Neither shield covers criminal acts, and courts can set aside a shield in some cases.</span></p>
<h3><strong>Who can form an LLP</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Rules vary by state. LegalZoom's business glossary notes that some states allow LLPs only for licensed professionals, such as law, accounting, and architecture or engineering firms.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A Wolters Kluwer page names California and New York as states that restrict LLPs to professionals.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The same page says Delaware and Texas are among the states that require insurance or an escrow account to cover liabilities.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Treat these as starting points and read your state's statute or call the Secretary of State, since the official state pages could not be checked for this article.</span></p>
<h3><strong>How an LLP is taxed</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">An LLP is a pass-through entity. The IRS says a partnership files Form 1065, pays no income tax itself, and passes profits and losses to the partners, who report their shares on their own returns.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The partnership gives each partner a Schedule K-1. Partners aren't employees, so they don't receive a W-2, and partners with self-employment income file Schedule SE and may make estimated tax payments (IRS, partnerships page, reviewed June 8, 2026).</span></p>

<h2><strong>Liability: The Difference That Matters Most</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Think of a two-partner accounting firm. One partner makes a costly error on a client's filing, and the client wins a judgment that exceeds the firm's assets.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">In an LLP, the other partner is generally protected from that judgment as long as she wasn't involved.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A single-member LLC doesn't apply here at all, because there is no second owner to protect you from.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">In a multi-member LLC, protection from a co-owner's professional errors can depend on your state's law and on whether the LLC is a professional one, so professionals in an LLC should ask an attorney how their state treats this.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Now flip the example. The firm owes $250,000 on an office lease and can't pay. Under a full-shield LLP or an LLC, the partners' personal assets are generally safe from that debt.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Under a partial-shield LLP, the partners could be personally liable. That is why the "full" or "partial" label matters more than the entity name.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Also watch the paperwork. LegalZoom's glossary warns that missing an annual report can cause an LLP to lose its status, which exposes partners to unlimited personal liability.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Mark every renewal date in your calendar.</span></p>
<h2><strong>Taxes: More Alike Than Different</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Most people expect a big tax gap between the two. In practice, a multi-member LLC and an LLP both default to partnership taxation, so the forms look nearly identical:&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Form 1065 for the entity, Schedule K-1 for each owner, and self-employment tax on active owners' earnings.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The real tax difference is flexibility. An LLC can file Form 8832 to be taxed as a corporation, which some owners explore once profits grow.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">An LLP is a partnership by design. If tax planning drives your decision, a CPA can model both paths with your actual numbers.</span></p>

<h2><strong>Which One Should You Choose?</strong></h2>
<h3><strong>Choose an LLC if you:</strong></h3>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Are a solo owner, since an LLP needs at least two partners</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Run an online store, consulting practice, agency, or rental property</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Want flexibility in management and profit splits</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">May want to elect corporate tax treatment later</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Work in a state that doesn't allow LLPs for your type of business</span></li>
</ul>
<h3><strong>Consider an LLP if you:</strong></h3>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Practice in a licensed profession with at least one partner</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Want each partner shielded from the others' professional mistakes</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Already run a general partnership and want to add protection without restructuring</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Work in a state that offers a full-shield LLP and allows your profession</span></li>
</ul>
<h3><strong>Talk to an attorney first if you:</strong></h3>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Practice law, medicine, accounting, architecture, or engineering, since many states regulate how professionals form entities</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Operate in more than one state</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Plan to raise outside investment, since investors often prefer other structures</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Have partners with unequal contributions or unusual buyout terms</span></li>
</ul>
<h2><strong>Can You Switch Later?</strong></h2>

<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Conversions can trigger filings, new registered agent paperwork, and tax questions, so it's cheaper to pick the right structure at the start.&nbsp;</span></p>

<h2><strong>Frequently Asked Questions</strong></h2>
<h3><strong>Is an LLP the same as an LLC?</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">No. Both limit personal liability, but an LLC can have one owner and is available to most businesses, while an LLP needs at least two partners and is often limited to licensed professionals.</span></p>
<h3><strong>Which is cheaper to form?</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">It depends on your state. The SBA says LLC registration is typically under $300. LLP costs vary by state, and some states add insurance or escrow requirements for LLPs, so check the state's fee schedule.</span></p>
<h3><strong>Can a single person form an LLP?</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">No. The SBA's comparison lists partnerships, including LLPs, as structures for two or more people. A solo owner who wants liability protection usually forms an LLC.</span></p>
<h3><strong>Do LLCs and LLPs pay the same taxes?</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">By default, a multi-member LLC and an LLP are both taxed as partnerships, so profits pass through to the owners' personal returns. A single-member LLC is disregarded for income tax, and an LLC can elect corporate treatment.</span></p>
<h3><strong>Does an LLP protect me from my own mistakes?</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">No. In both full-shield and partial-shield states, you stay liable for your own malpractice or misconduct. The protection covers other partners' mistakes and, in some states, the firm's business debts.</span></p>
<h3><strong>Do I need a lawyer to choose?</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">For a simple business, many owners choose an LLC without one. If you're in a licensed profession or have partners, an attorney can explain your state's rules, and a CPA can explain the tax side.</span></p>
<h2><strong>The Bottom Line</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Choose an LLC if you want a flexible structure that works for solo owners and groups in nearly any industry.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Consider an LLP if you're a licensed professional with partners and your state allows it, especially in a full-shield state.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Before you file, check three things: whether your state allows the structure for your profession, whether its liability shield is full or partial, and what annual filings keep that protection in place.</span></p>
